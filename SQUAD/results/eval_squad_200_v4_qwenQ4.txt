Answer: The halogen bulb decreases darkening over time through the halogen cycle, a process that involves the use of halogen gas and the tungsten filament. In a halogen lamp, the tungsten filament, which is made of tungsten (a material with a high melting point), is enclosed in a quartz envelope filled with a mixture of halogen gases, typically iodine or bromine, at low pressure. This environment allows for a more efficient operation of the bulb.

During operation, some of the tungsten from the filament evaporates and deposits onto the inner surface of the envelope, causing it to darken. However, the halogen cycle prevents this darkening. As the tungsten evaporates, it forms tungsten halides, which are compounds of tungsten and the halogen gas. These tungsten halides are then redeposited back onto the filament, effectively recycling the tungsten and maintaining the filament's efficiency and brightness. This process significantly increases the bulb's lifetime compared to traditional incandescent bulbs, which do not have this mechanism and experience more rapid filament evaporation and envelope darkening.
